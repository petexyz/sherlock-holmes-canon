Case Duration

How long each case lasts, from the Canon alone

Some cases are over in an evening; others keep Holmes waiting for months. Each adventure is timed from the moment Holmes is drawn in to the moment the case is closed, by counting the days as Watson tells them. Earlier events and later scenes are noted but not counted. Where the length depends on a choice of where the case begins or ends, a numbered note under the table gives the other reading. Judgement calls

  • [1] The Gloria Scott: Counted from Holmes's first visit to Donnithorpe, when Hudson appears. Counted from the telegram that recalls him, the case lasts about a day.
  • [2] A Study in Scarlet: Counted through Holmes's closing explanation. If the case ends with the arrest and confession, it is 2 days.
  • [3] The Five Orange Pips: The long wait for news of the Lone Star is counted, since the loss of the ship is what closes the case. The active case, from Openshaw's visit to the posting of the pips, is 2 days.
  • [4] The Crooked Man: Counted from Holmes's start on the Tuesday morning. Watson is with him only from the Wednesday night, about a day and a half.
  • [5] The Man with the Twisted Lip: Holmes is already at work when Watson finds him, and the text never says when he was called in; the day after the disappearance is assumed. Watson's own part is a single night.
  • [6] The Blue Carbuncle: Counted from Christmas morning, when Peterson brings the hat and the goose. Counted from the finding of the stone, it is about half a day.
  • [7] The Dying Detective: Counted from Watson's arrival at the sick-room. Counted from the day Holmes took to his bed, it is about 3 days.
  • [8] Black Peter: Counted from the murder, since Holmes says the case has haunted him for ten days. Counted from Hopkins's call at Baker Street, it is 3 days.
  • [9] The Illustrious Client: Counted to the newspaper notice that the marriage is off. If the case ends on the night at Vernon Lodge, it is 11 days.
  • [10] His Last Bow: Counted as Holmes's two years under cover as Altamont. The action on the page is a single evening, 2 August 1914.

The Gloria Scott about 3 months[1] · the text leaves a gap

Holmes goes down to Donnithorpe for a month of the long vacation; early in the stay Trevor faints at his deduction, and on the day before Holmes leaves the sailor Hudson appears. Holmes spends seven weeks in London, and when autumn is far advanced Victor Trevor's telegram recalls him; he arrives the evening Old Trevor dies and reads the Gloria Scott papers that night. The count from the start of the visit is roughly a month plus about two months, with the weeks only roughly given. (60 to 120 days possible)

  • Before“It was the year ’55 when the Crimean war was at its height” The convict voyage of the Gloria Scott, from which all the backstory is counted.
  • Day 1“and I accepted his hospitality for a month of the long vacation” Holmes goes to Donnithorpe for a month of the summer vacation, where the case begins.
  • Day 2“One evening, shortly after my arrival, we were sitting over a glass of port after dinner” Holmes's deduction of the initials J. A. makes Old Trevor faint, so the matter opens within the first days of the visit.
  • Day 28“On the very day, however, before I left, an incident occurred which proved in the sequel to be of importance.” Hudson arrives on the day before Holmes leaves, near the end of the month's stay.
  • Day 29“All this occurred during the first month of the long vacation.” The visit and Hudson's first appearance fall within the first month of the vacation.
  • Day 29“I went up to my London rooms, where I spent seven weeks working out a few experiments in organic chemistry.” A waiting period of seven weeks, from about Day 29 to about Day 78, during which Holmes has no part in the Trevor matter.
  • Day 88“when the autumn was far advanced and the vacation drawing to a close” Trevor's telegram arrives late in the autumn; Holmes sets off the same day.
  • Day 88“I saw at a glance that the last two months had been very trying ones for him” The last two months of the case are counted from Hudson's visit, which confirms about 60 days from Day 28 to the evening of the death.
  • Day 88“That was the narrative which I read that night to young Trevor” Holmes reads the papers aloud the night of Old Trevor's death, and the case is resolved.

Afterwards

  • “went out to the Terai tea planting, where I hear that he is doing well” Victor Trevor later goes out to the Terai as a tea planter, and Holmes hears he is doing well; the date is not given.

Before the case

  • “For more than twenty years we have led peaceful and useful lives” The convict voyage and the mutiny of the Gloria Scott were in autumn 1855, over twenty years before Hudson reappeared; the text also gives thirty years in two places, which is inconsistent with the other figure.

Told later

  • “as we sat one winter’s night on either side of the fire” Holmes tells the story to Watson on a winter's night at Baker Street, some years after the case; it is his first case, told after Watson has begun to collect his cases.

The Musgrave Ritual about 10 hours · one or two small assumptions

Musgrave calls on Holmes in Montague Street one morning, about a week after the Thursday-night episode with Brunton and the morning after the county police's last search. They take the first train to Sussex, arrive that afternoon, and Holmes measures the shadow with the sun low, finds the cellar and the body at sunset, and explains the crown as dusk falls with the moon up. The whole case is one day, with no clock time given for the start. (0.3 to 0.5 days possible)

  • Before“One day last week—on Thursday night, to be more exact” Musgrave catches Brunton in the library on a Thursday night, about a week or less before he visits Holmes.
  • Before“On the third morning, however he did not appear” Brunton vanishes on the third morning after the Thursday night, so the third morning falls on a Sunday if the two days are Friday and Saturday.
  • Before“On the third night after Brunton’s disappearance, the nurse” Rachel Howells vanishes on the third night after Brunton, and the mere is dragged afterwards.
  • Before“although we made every possible search and inquiry yesterday” Musgrave's last day of searching is the day before he calls on Holmes.
  • Day 1“until one morning he walked into my room in Montague Street” Musgrave arrives one morning, and Holmes is first drawn into the matter.
  • Day 1“The same afternoon saw us both at Hurlstone.” Holmes and Musgrave take the first train down and reach Hurlstone that afternoon.
  • Day 1“in less than an hour it would lie just above the topmost branches of the old oak” Holmes watches the sun, low in the sky; the measurement is made when the sun reaches the oak top, late afternoon or evening.
  • Day 1“The setting sun shone full upon the passage floor” The sun is setting when Holmes reaches the spot in the passage, so this is near sunset.
  • Day 1“For twenty minutes I had sat motionless, thinking the matter out.” Holmes sits twenty minutes in the cellar after the body is found, after sunset.
  • Day 1“The twilight had closed in and the moon was shining brightly in the sky before my narrative was finished.” Holmes finishes explaining the crown after dark, so the case closes that evening.

Afterwards

  • “They have the crown down at Hurlstone” The crown is kept at Hurlstone after some legal bother and a sum paid; the time is not given.

Before the case

  • “is in the spelling of the middle of the seventeenth century” The ritual and the crown go back to the 1640s-50s, the middle of the seventeenth century, over two centuries before the case.

Told later

  • “One winter’s night, as we sat together by the fire” Holmes tells the story to Watson one winter's night at Baker Street while sorting his papers, after they have been living together for some time; the interval since the case is not given.

A Study in Scarlet 3 days[2] · one or two small assumptions

Day 1 is 4 March: Gregson's note arrives at breakfast, Holmes and Watson visit Lauriston Gardens, question Rance, and set the ring trap that evening, ending near midnight. Day 2 opens with the newspapers, then Lestrade reports Stangerson dead at about six that morning, and Holmes arrests Jefferson Hope in the sitting-room; Hope confesses at the station that day. Hope's aneurism bursts that night, he is found dead on the Thursday morning (Day 3), and Holmes explains his method to Watson the following evening, which this reading takes as the evening of Day 3. (2 to 4 days possible)

  • Before“Died August 4th, 1860.” Backstory marker: the grave-board of John Ferrier in Utah, the only dated event of the Utah narrative, about twenty years before the case.
  • Before“They had been seen together at Euston Station about half-past eight on the evening of the third.” Drebber and Stangerson are last seen together on the evening before the case opens; Drebber is dead at Lauriston Gardens in the small hours of the 4th, before Holmes is drawn in.
  • Day 1“It was upon the 4th of March, as I have good reason to remember, that I rose somewhat earlier than usual,” Watson's own date for the morning of the summons; no year is given.
  • Day 1“There has been a bad business during the night at 3, Lauriston Gardens, off the Brixton Road.” Gregson's note, brought by the commissionaire at breakfast, draws Holmes in. It asks him to come "any time before twelve", and the body was found at about two that morning.
  • Day 1“It was one o’clock when we left No. 3, Lauriston Gardens.” Holmes and Watson have spent the morning at the house; they go on to Rance in Kennington, then Holmes to Halle's concert in the afternoon.
  • Day 1“Apply Dr. Watson, 221B, Baker Street, between eight and nine this evening.” Holmes's Found advertisement for the wedding ring fixes the evening; Watson notes at eight o'clock that the visitor is due.
  • Day 1“It was close upon twelve before I heard the sharp sound of his latch-key.” Holmes returns from tailing the supposed old woman (Mrs. Sawyer), who gave him the slip, so Day 1 ends at about midnight.
  • Day 2“The papers next day were full of the” The next morning's newspapers report the Brixton Mystery; Holmes and Watson read them at breakfast. The Standard's 'Tuesday, the 4th inst.' agrees with the 4th as Day 1 but not with the weekday of 4 March 1881.
  • Day 2“was murdered at Halliday’s Private Hotel about six o’clock this morning.” Lestrade brings news of Stangerson's murder during Gregson's visit; he reached the hotel at eight, so this is the morning of Day 2.
  • Day 2“let me introduce you to Mr. Jefferson Hope, the murderer of Enoch Drebber and of Joseph Stangerson.” Wiggins brings the cab to Baker Street the same morning and Holmes handcuffs Hope; the arrest and his confession at the police station follow that day (Hope himself says he cabbed 'a day or so' more, which does not fit).
  • Day 2“On Thursday the prisoner will be brought before the magistrates, and your attendance will be required.” The Inspector sets the hearing for the Thursday, the next day but one counting from Tuesday the 4th.
  • Day 3“On the very night after his capture the aneurism burst, and he was found in the morning stretched upon the floor of the cell,” Hope dies the night after his arrest and is found on the Thursday morning, so the case is closed by his death and no testimony is needed.
  • Day 3“as we chatted it over next evening.” Holmes's closing explanation and the Echo paragraph come the evening after Hope's death, read here as that Thursday evening; if it means the day after that, the span is 4 days.
  • Day 3“was able to lay my hand upon the criminal within three days.” Holmes's own figure, which fits a count from the night of the murder (3 March) to the arrest on the 5th.

Before the case

  • “That girl that I spoke of was to have married me twenty years ago.” The Utah narrative of Part II begins on 4 May 1847 (Ferrier and Lucy found on the Alkali Plain) and reaches the grave of John Ferrier, dated 4 August 1860. Hope then spends some months in the mountains, nearly five years at the Nevada mines, and years more tracking Drebber and Stangerson across America and Europe to London, so the grievance reaches back about twenty years (about thirty-four years to the first Utah scene). Watson's own Afghan service, with Maiwand, enteric fever and nine months' leave, precedes the case by a few months.

Told later

  • “I have all the facts in my journal, and the public shall know them.” Watson writes it up from his journal and Lestrade's notebook as a reprint from his Reminiscences. Part II is introduced as Hope's account recorded in that journal. No interval is given between the case and the writing.

The Resident Patient 2 days · every day accounted for

Dr Trevelyan calls at Baker Street at about ten on an October night, and Holmes goes back with him to Brook Street the same night. The next morning at 7.30 Trevelyan sends for them, Blessington is found hanged, and Holmes returns at a quarter to four that afternoon with the identity of the gang. The case spans one night and the following day, which is 2 days.

  • Before“It was boisterous October weather” Watson places the case in October, though he says the exact date is uncertain.
  • Before“Some weeks ago Mr. Blessington came down to me in, as it seemed to me, a state of considerable agitation.” Blessington's first fright, some weeks before, is part of the backstory.
  • Before“Two days ago I received the letter which I now read to you.” The Russian's first appointment, set for the following evening, is made two days before the case; it is Dr Trevelyan's account, not Holmes's.
  • Day 1“It was ten o’clock before we reached Baker Street again.” Holmes and Watson find Dr Trevelyan waiting at ten that evening, and Holmes is first drawn in.
  • Day 1“at the very same hour this evening, they both came marching into my consulting-room” The second visit by the Russian and his son took place that evening at a quarter past six, the same day, and led Trevelyan to come.
  • Day 1“Within a quarter of an hour we had been dropped at the door of the physician’s residence in Brook Street” Holmes follows the doctor to Brook Street the same night.
  • Day 2“At half-past seven next morning, in the first glimmer of daylight” Holmes wakes Watson at 7.30 the next morning, which starts the second day.
  • Day 2“When the maid entered, about seven, there the unfortunate fellow was hanging in the middle of the room.” Blessington was found hanged at about seven in the morning.
  • Day 2““I’ll be back by three,” said he, when we had finished our meal.” Holmes goes out after breakfast to investigate and promises to return by three.
  • Day 2“but it was a quarter to four before my friend put in an appearance” Holmes comes back with the gang's identity at a quarter to four and explains everything, so the case is closed that afternoon.

Afterwards

  • “which was lost some years ago with all hands upon the Portuguese coast” Years later the three men are thought to have drowned in the steamer Norah Creina, lost off the Portuguese coast; the date is only 'some years ago'.

Before the case

  • “This was in 1875.” The Worthingdon bank robbery was in 1875; the three men got fifteen years and were released early, so the earlier events reach back some years.

Told later

  • “I cannot be sure of the exact date, for some of my memoranda upon the matter have been mislaid” Watson writes it up some years afterwards, with part of his notes lost, and cannot give the exact date.

The Speckled Band 2 days · one or two small assumptions

Helen Stoner wakes Holmes and Watson at a quarter-past seven on a morning in early April 1883 (Day 1). They visit Stoke Moran that afternoon, spend the evening at the Crown Inn and keep the night vigil in her bedroom, and Roylott dies in the small hours after three, which falls on Day 2. The only loose end is Holmes's own explanation, given 'as we travelled back next day', which is Day 2 on the most natural reading and could be Day 3. (2 to 3 days possible)

  • Before“Two days ago some repairs were started in the west wing of the building” Helen moved into her dead sister's room two days before she comes to Baker Street; the whistle began again the night before Day 1.
  • Day 1“It was early in April in the year ’83 that I woke one morning to find Sherlock Holmes standing, fully dressed, by the side of my bed.” Watson states the month and year and the case begins the same morning.
  • Day 1“as the clock on the mantelpiece showed me that it was only a quarter-past seven” Holmes is drawn in at 7.15 a.m. when the client arrives.
  • Day 1“It was nearly one o’clock when Sherlock Holmes returned from his excursion.” Roylott's visit and Holmes's trip to Doctors' Commons fill the morning; they leave for Waterloo after one.
  • Day 1“At dusk we saw Dr. Grimesby Roylott drive past” Holmes and Watson are at the Crown Inn by evening, after the afternoon inspection of the house.
  • Day 1“Two hours passed slowly away, and then, suddenly, just at the stroke of eleven, a single bright light shone out right in front of us.” Miss Stoner's lamp signal at eleven sends them across the grounds into her room.
  • Day 2“Twelve struck, and one and two and three, and still we sat waiting silently for whatever might befall.” The vigil runs past midnight; the snake and Roylott's death come after three in the morning, so on Day 2 by the calendar.
  • Day 2“how we conveyed her by the morning train to the care of her good aunt at Harrow” Helen is taken to Harrow by the morning train after the night's events.
  • Day 2“The little which I had yet to learn of the case was told me by Sherlock Holmes as we travelled back next day.” Holmes's explanation closes the case on the journey back to London; 'next day' is read as the day after the night, Day 2, but could be the day after the morning train to Harrow (Day 3).

Afterwards

  • “of how the slow process of official inquiry came to the conclusion that the doctor met his fate while indiscreetly playing with a dangerous pet” An official inquiry follows some time after the death and concludes that Roylott was killed by a dangerous pet; Watson was released from his promise of secrecy only when Helen Stoner died years later.

Before the case

  • “She died just two years ago, and it is of her death that I wish to speak to you.” The events that bear on the case reach back about two years to Julia Stoner's death on the eve of her wedding; the family history goes back further (mother killed eight years before, Roylott's years in Calcutta, the twins aged two at the remarriage, roughly thirty years).

Told later

  • “On glancing over my notes of the seventy odd cases in which I have during the last eight years studied the methods of my friend Sherlock Holmes” Watson writes it up years afterwards, only after Helen Stoner's death released him from his promise; he speaks of his notes on seventy odd cases over the last eight years.

The Yellow Face about 6 hours · one or two small assumptions

On an early-spring afternoon Holmes and Watson return to Baker Street at nearly five and find Munro has called and left. He returns, tells his story, and the pair go down to Norbury by the seven o'clock train, enter the cottage that dark night and learn the truth. Holmes makes his closing remark late that night, so the adventure is about six hours. (0.2 to 0.3 days possible)

  • Before“Well, last Monday evening I was taking a stroll down that way” Munro first sees the face at the cottage on the Monday before Day 1.
  • Before“What on this earth could my wife be doing out on the country road at three in the morning?” Effie's night walk is in the early hours of Tuesday.
  • Before“On the third day, however, I had ample evidence that her solemn promise was not enough to hold her back” Two quiet days after Tuesday, then the cottage found empty on the third, Friday.
  • Before“That was yesterday, Mr. Holmes, and I have not seen her since” The third-day scene took place the day before Day 1, so Day 1 is the Saturday.
  • Day 1“One day in early spring he had so far relaxed as to go for a walk with me in the Park” Season and the afternoon walk on Day 1.
  • Day 1“It was nearly five before we were back in Baker Street once more.” Holmes learns of the visitor, and Munro himself returns soon after; the adventure starts at about five.
  • Day 1“Suddenly this morning it occurred to me that you were the man to advise me” Munro had come to Baker Street earlier the same day.
  • Day 1“It came just as we had finished our tea.” Munro's telegram reaches Baker Street in the early evening.
  • Day 1“Will meet the seven o’clock train, and will take no steps until you arrive.” They reach Norbury about seven and enter the cottage that dark, rainy night.
  • Day 1“Not another word did he say of the case until late that night” Holmes's remark about 'Norbury' comes late that night, closing the adventure.

Before the case

  • “For three years I have kept her existence a secret from you” Munro's week of trouble runs from Monday to Friday before Day 1; Effie's marriage to Munro is three years old, and her first marriage to John Hebron and the child's birth in Atlanta go further back still.

The Reigate Squire about 5 hours · one or two small assumptions

The party reaches Reigate a week after the return from Lyons, and on the arrival evening Holmes hears of the Acton burglary but does not mean to meddle. The Cunningham murder happens that night, and next morning at breakfast Inspector Forrester asks for Holmes's help, which starts the case. Holmes arrests the Cunninghams that morning and gives his explanation soon after one o'clock, so the case itself takes about five hours; counting from the evening before would make it about 17 hours. (0.15 to 0.7 days possible)

  • Before“it was upon the 14th of April that I received a telegram from Lyons” The telegram to Watson, a Thursday, starts the run-up; Holmes is ill and the matter in Reigate has not yet begun.
  • Before“Three days later we were back in Baker Street together” They return to London about Monday 18 April; the Acton burglary takes place the same Monday.
  • Before“a week after our return from Lyons we were under the Colonel’s roof” They reach Reigate on the evening of Monday 25 April, and the Colonel mentions the Acton burglary after dinner.
  • Before“It was quarter to twelve when the alarm broke out.” William Kirwan is shot at 11.45 that night, after the party goes to bed; Holmes is not yet involved.
  • Day 1“next morning the problem obtruded itself upon us” The next morning the problem forces itself on them at breakfast, and the Inspector's visit draws Holmes in.
  • Day 1“I will be with you again in half an hour.” Holmes goes off with the Inspector to the scene.
  • Day 1“An hour and half had elapsed before the Inspector returned alone.” The Inspector comes back after an hour and a half, still the same morning, and takes the others to the Cunninghams'.
  • Day 1“at about a quarter to one on Tuesday morning an attempt was made” Holmes's deliberately faulty reward notice names Tuesday morning, which dates the case day as Tuesday 26 April.
  • Day 1“for about one o’clock he rejoined us in the Colonel’s smoking-room” Holmes returns with Mr. Acton after arresting the Cunninghams and gives his explanation, which closes the case early in the afternoon.

Before the case

  • “they’ve had a lawsuit for some years” The Cunninghams and Acton have been in a lawsuit for some years, and the burglary at Acton's, a week earlier, was the first act; Holmes's Lyons case ran over two months before.

Told later

  • “are too recent in the minds of the public” Watson writes it up from his notes some time later, when the Netherland-Sumatra business is still fresh in the public mind; no interval is given.

The Five Orange Pips about 2 months[3] · the text leaves a gap

John Openshaw comes to Baker Street on a stormy evening in the last days of September 1887, the day after his pips arrive, and is dead in the Thames that night. Next day Holmes traces the barque Lone Star and posts his own five pips to her captain, so the active part of the case takes two days. It closes only when the Lone Star is lost in the gales, after a wait the text calls 'long' without giving a length, and the 'about 2 months' is an estimate within that gap. (2 to 180 days possible)

  • Before“It was headed, “March, 1869,”” The colonel's diary page dates the Klan business to March 1869, about eighteen years before the case.
  • Before“The letter arrived on March 10, 1883.” First pips and Elias Openshaw's death, seven weeks later on 2 May 1883; backstory.
  • Before“It was in January, ’85, that my poor father met his end, and two years and eight months have elapsed since then.” Joseph Openshaw dies in January 1885, so the present is two years and eight months later, in September 1887.
  • Before“yesterday morning the blow fell in the very shape in which it had come upon my father.” Openshaw's own pips arrived the morning before his visit. Holmes later says 'two days', which does not match 'yesterday morning'; this does not alter the count.
  • Day 1“It was in the latter days of September, and the equinoctial gales had set in with exceptional violence.” Holmes is drawn in on a stormy evening in late September; Openshaw calls and tells his tale.
  • Day 1“To-morrow I shall set to work upon your case.” Openshaw leaves a little before nine; Holmes plans to begin next day.
  • Day 2“It had cleared in the morning, and the sun was shining with a subdued brightness” Morning after the visit; the paper reports Openshaw drowned the previous night between nine and ten.
  • Day 2“It was nearly ten o’clock before he entered, looking pale and worn.” Holmes returns at night after a day over the shipping registers and posts the pips to Captain Calhoun of the Lone Star, which sailed that morning for Savannah.
  • Weeks later“We waited long for news of the Lone Star of Savannah, but none ever reached us.” The case stays open while Holmes waits for news of the ship; the text gives no length, only that the wait was long.
  • Weeks later“We did at last hear that somewhere far out in the Atlantic a shattered stern-post of a boat was seen” Word of wreckage marked 'L. S.' ends the matter, at a date the text does not give; the equinoctial gales that year are blamed.

Before the case

  • “It was headed, “March, 1869,”” The Openshaw feud reaches back to the colonel's time with the Ku Klux Klan in America, with a diary page of March 1869, about eighteen years before the case.

Told later

  • “When I glance over my notes and records of the Sherlock Holmes cases between the years ’82 and ’90” Watson writes it up after 1890, since he is looking back over his notes for the years 1882 to 1890; the interval is not given.

The Noble Bachelor about 8 hours · one or two small assumptions

Holmes opens Lord St. Simon's letter at about three on an autumn afternoon, a few days after the wedding. Lord St. Simon calls just after four and Lestrade follows, Holmes goes out after five and returns shortly before nine, and the Moultons and St. Simon arrive in the evening. The explanation ends the case that night, so the adventure runs about eight hours. (0.29 to 0.4 days possible)

  • Before“Two days later—that is, on Wednesday last—there is a curt announcement that the wedding had taken place” The wedding was on a Wednesday of the week before the consultation, shortly before Day 1; the bride vanished at the breakfast.
  • Day 1“I will call at four o’clock in the afternoon” The letter from Lord St. Simon announces his call; Holmes has come home from an afternoon stroll to find it.
  • Day 1“It is three now.” Holmes is drawn in at about three o'clock, an hour before the client arrives.
  • Day 1“as the clock makes it a few minutes after four” Lord St. Simon arrives and is questioned.
  • Day 1“It was after five o’clock when Sherlock Holmes left me” Holmes goes out after Lestrade leaves, to find the Moultons.
